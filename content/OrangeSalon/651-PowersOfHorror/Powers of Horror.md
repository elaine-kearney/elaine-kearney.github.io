# Chapter 1 Approaching Abjection
## Neither Subject Nor Object

We're dealing here with the [[subject object distinction]] and how it relates to the abject.

What is the subject object distinction?  Subject and object are both grammatical as well as philosophical terms, and it can be helpful to understand the latter through the former.  Subjects are beings which do, which can think and act.  Objects are, of course, those things which are acted upon, or referred to.  It can be helpful to loosely think of it as the distinction between first and third person.  Importantly, what is a subject in one context might be an object in another.  You have your own thoughts and interiority, but I can only ever grasp you through what I see in front of me.  The context in which we are using subject/object here is in relation to *consciousness*.  The subject is the conscious being, while the object is the object *of consciousness*.  The object of consciousness is what consciousness is *about*.  Any thought or sensation that we may have is always *about* something.  In the tradition of analytic philosophy, this property of consciousness as being *about something* is referred to as [[intentionality]], and it is seen as a fundamental feature of consciousness.

Kristeva is writing in the [[psychoanalysis|psychoanalytic]] tradition.  One of the primary features of psychoanalysis is its emphasis on a developmental view on [[consciousness]] and its conception of the unconscious.  Accordingly, consciousness and intentionality are not taken for granted, but are considered as emerging out of complex social and developmental processes as essentially useful fictions or half-truths that serve to represent or signify more fundamental animal or ontological drives.  Psychoanalysis does not take the self-presentation of consciousness at face value, and so it is possible to speak of the "motion" or "movement" of objects in consciousness.

The abject takes this destabilization of the subject/object distinction further by positing the existence of something 'like' an object but which is not an object, which is defined in fact by not being an object, the (a)bject.  The question is why the abject is not an object.  Kristeva describes this as 'jettisoning' or 'radical exclusion,' and attributes this exclusion to the superego.  We'll see later on that the superego is actually the primary source of abjection.

## The Improper / Unclean

This is probably the most difficult section of the chapter because Kristeva is giving a psychoanalytic, developmental account of abjection here without explicitly stating that that is what she is doing.

The section on food-loathing and the interaction with the milk-skin is written from the perspective of a child being fed by their parents.  The child establishes herself as individual by the rejection of the parent's will, through abjecting herself.  By vomiting out the food or disgusting substance, the child separates herself from the will and desires of the parent.  The child herself becomes disgusting in order to establish herself as herself, outside of the parents desires or symbolic system.

### Terminology
**Signified / Signifier** - Coming from semiotics, heavily used by Lacan, the signifier / signified distinction is the distinction between a representation and what it represents.  Importantly, for many semioticians there is no ultimate signified.  Rather, signs always refer to other signs, and it is in this circularity that meaning is established.  Words, pictures, sounds, gestures, anything can be a signifier.  For Lacan, the phallus is the stand in for the master signfiier

## Beyond the Unconscious

Kristeva is here talking about some of the implications of her conception of abjection for psychoanalytic theorization and practice.  She posits that, just as neurosis and psychosis stem from disorders in desire, disorders in abjection create their own form of pathology.  At root is the contrast she draws between object and abject.  Since abjection blurs the subject-object distinction (since the abject do not rise to the level of objects,) the standard methods of understanding pathology fall away, since at root they are based on disorders in desire, which is always desire for an object (the *objet petit a*).  Because of this, disorders in abjection short-circuit the typical repressive frameworks, leading to statements of desires that would typically be unconscious.

This also furthers our understanding of abjection, emphasizing its method of operation - exclusion, in contrast with negation.

# Chapter 2 Something to be Scared Of
## The Object as Trimming of Anguish

*"From the start, fear and object are linked"*

Kristeva is setting out the goal for the chapter: to examine how the abject, in its functional role as that which threatens objectification, interacts with and critiques the Freudian [[oedipus complex|Oedipal]] developmental framework.

Before that though, Kristeva first points out that it is important to take a developmental view of [[the object]] itself.  She points out that logically, the object must be preceded by something like [[partial object|partial objects]], that is, objects understood as the aims of drives -- as she puts it:

> Do we not find, sooner (chronologically and logically speak- ing), if not objects at least pre-objects, poles of attraction of a demand for air, food, and motion?

Through these semi-objects it is clear that the time preceding the development of the notion of the Oedipal object is rife with differentiation, or as she calls it, *gradations within modalities of separation.*  This is sort of like a pre-symbolic, pre-lingual, pre-cognitive experience of suffering that the child endures, culminating in [[castration]].  Seen in this way, the Oedipus complex, in which the whole drama of psychic development is inscribed, is actually a particular developmental form stemming from the process of objectification.
